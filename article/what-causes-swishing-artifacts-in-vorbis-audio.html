What Causes Swishing Artifacts in Vorbis Audio?

The "swishing" artifact in low-bitrate Vorbis files is a perceptual audio distortion characterized by a distinct watery, flanging, or sweeping noise overlaid onto high-frequency sounds. This article explains the technical mechanisms behind this artifact, exploring how psychoacoustic bit-allocation compromises, coarse quantization of MDCT coefficients, stereo coupling methods, and spectral floor modeling interact to produce this unnatural acoustic effect.

Understanding the Swishing Sound

In lossy audio compression, the goal is to discard data that the human auditory system is least likely to perceive. When the Ogg Vorbis codec is forced to operate at very low bitrates (typically below 80–96 kbps for stereo content), it exhausts its available data budget.

Listeners perceive the resulting artifact as a phasey, unstable "swish" or "underwater" sound. It is most prominent in audio containing complex high frequencies, rapid transients, or dense harmonic structures—such as cymbals, applause, brass instruments, and vocal sibilance ("s" and "t" sounds).

Root Causes of the Artifact

The artifact is not caused by a single bug, but rather by a combination of encoding strategies reaching their limits under extreme data constraints.

1. Coarse Quantization and Spectral Hole-Filling

Vorbis uses the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) to convert time-domain audio samples into frequency-domain representations. At very low bitrates, there are not enough bits to encode the precise amplitude of every frequency band. The encoder aggressively quantizes (rounds off) or outright zeroes out high-frequency MDCT coefficients.

Because these coefficients fluctuate wildly between adjacent analysis frames, the high-frequency energy rapidly drops in and out from one block to the next. The ear interprets this rapid, inconsistent modulation of high-frequency energy as a moving, swishing noise floor rather than static hiss.

2. Channel Coupling (Stereo Phase Smearing)

To maximize efficiency at low bitrates, Vorbis uses channel coupling techniques, such as point stereo and phase coupling. Instead of storing independent left and right audio channels, the encoder combines them into a shared spectral representation and transmits directional vectors or energy ratios.

At low bitrates, the phase relationships between channels become smeared or lost. When the decoder reconstructs the stereo field, minute phase discrepancies fluctuate over time. This phase instability creates an acoustic comb-filtering effect, identical to a flanger or phaser audio effect, which contributes heavily to the perception of sound "swishing" across the stereo field.

3. Spectral Floor Approximation

Vorbis separates audio spectrum encoding into two parts: the "floor" (the overall spectral envelope/shape) and the "residue" (the detailed spectral peaks and valleys). In low-bitrate modes, the floor representation is simplified to conserve bits.

If the encoded floor cannot adapt smoothly to fast temporal changes or sharp tonal peaks, it imparts an artificial temporal modulation to the residual noise. As this envelope changes shape frame by frame, it sweeps across frequency regions, reinforcing the sweeping, watery quality.

4. Time-Frequency Resolution Limits (Block Switching)

Vorbis switches between short and long MDCT block sizes to balance time resolution (for transients) and frequency resolution (for steady tones). At low bitrates, the decision thresholds for switching blocks become constrained by the bit budget. When a short block cannot be used due to bit starvation, transient energy smears over a longer time window (pre- and post-echo), diffusing the energy of the transient into a prolonged swish.

Mitigation and Prevention

The swishing artifact is an inherent consequence of operating Vorbis below its intended quality threshold. Several approaches resolve or minimize the issue:

  • Increase the Bitrate: Vorbis was designed primarily for variable bitrate (VBR) operation. Setting the quality scale to -q 4 or higher (approximately 128 kbps stereo) generally provides sufficient bandwidth to eliminate audible phase smearing and spectral holes.
  • Use Tuned Encoders: Tuning branches, such as aoTuV (Aoyumi's Tuned Vorbis), modified the psychoacoustic algorithms and floor-handling parameters of the reference libvorbis library, significantly reducing swishing artifacts at lower bitrates.
  • Transition to Modern Codecs: Codecs such as Opus (which combines technologies from Skype's SILK and Xiph.Org's CELT) incorporate superior noise-band synthesis, transient handling, and prediction techniques, allowing clean audio reproduction at bitrates where Vorbis exhibits pronounced swishing.
