Why WebM Originally Adopted Vorbis Audio

When Google launched the WebM project in May 2010 to create a royalty-free media format for the web, it paired the VP8 video codec with Vorbis audio inside a Matroska-based container. WebM adopted Vorbis primarily because it was a mature, patent-unencumbered, open-source audio standard capable of competing with proprietary formats like AAC. At the time of WebM's launch, Vorbis was the only fully realized, widely tested, royalty-free audio codec available that aligned with the open-web initiative.

Commitment to Royalty-Free Licensing

The primary objective behind the WebM project was to provide an open alternative to the proprietary H.264 and AAC media standards, which required expensive licensing fees through patent pools like MPEG LA. Because Google aimed to make WebM natively supported by all modern web browsers without legal or financial barriers, every component of the container had to be open-source. Vorbis, developed by the Xiph.Org Foundation, was completely open, well-documented, and free of known patent claims, making it a natural fit for WebM's licensing goals.

Codec Maturity and Reliability

By 2010, Vorbis had been actively developed and maintained for nearly a decade. Unlike experimental codecs, Vorbis was a battle-tested format with stable reference implementations, predictable CPU usage, and widespread developer familiarity. It provided perceptual audio quality that rivaled or exceeded MP3 and approached AAC at standard bitrates, ensuring that WebM files would deliver acceptable compression efficiency without sacrificing sound fidelity.

Existing Browser and Software Support

Key open-web browsers, including Mozilla Firefox and Opera, had already integrated native Vorbis support for the HTML5 <audio> and <video> tags using the Ogg container. By adopting Vorbis for WebM, browser vendors did not need to build or license a completely new audio decoding pipeline. They only had to integrate support for demuxing the WebM container (a subset of Matroska) and feed the audio stream into their existing Vorbis decoders. This significantly accelerated the adoption of WebM across major web platforms.

The Absence of the Opus Codec

Modern WebM implementations heavily favor the Opus audio codec, but Opus did not exist in a finalized state in 2010. The IETF work that merged Skype's SILK codec with Xiph.Org's CELT codec to create Opus was still underway. Opus was not officially standardized until RFC 6716 was published in September 2012. Until Opus matured and gained industry consensus, Vorbis stood as the only production-ready, open-source audio codec capable of serving the needs of the WebM project.
