Vorbis Stereo Coupling via Floor Matrix Explained

Vorbis implements stereo coupling by separating an audio signal's spectral energy envelope—known as the floor—from its fine harmonic structure, referred to as the residue. Instead of applying traditional Mid/Side (M/S) stereo transforms directly to the transformed frequency spectrum, Vorbis performs polar coupling on the residue vectors and scales the reconstructed vectors using independent channel floor curves defined in the mapping stage. This approach preserves channel separation, avoids phase cancellation, and significantly reduces the bitrate required to encode multi-channel audio.

The Floor and Residue Architecture

To understand coupling in Vorbis, the audio pipeline must first be split into its two primary frequency components:

  1. The Floor: A low-resolution representation of the spectral envelope that models the psychoacoustic masking threshold of the human ear. It is encoded either via linear predictive coding (Floor 0) or piecewise linear synthesis using line segments (Floor 1).
  2. The Residue: The high-resolution spectral details (the fine harmonics) remaining after dividing the raw Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) spectrum by the floor envelope.

Because the floor envelope models the broad distribution of acoustic energy, the residue vector contains normalized, noise-like spectral data with unit variance.

Coupling at the Mapping Stage

Vorbis defines channel coupling inside Mapping 0, the core structural mapping mechanism specified by the format. Channel coupling does not take place in the time domain or directly on MDCT coefficients. Instead, it occurs entirely on the residue vectors before spectral synthesis.

The mapping configuration contains an array of coupling steps. Each coupling step binds two channels together: a magnitude channel and an angle channel.

  • The magnitude vector (\(M\)) captures the dominant energy present between the two channels at each spectral bin.
  • The angle vector (\(A\)) describes the relative distribution or phase difference between the original channels.

During encoding, the two stereo residue channels (\(R_A\) and \(R_B\)) are mapped into these polar coordinates. This polar stereo mapping ensures that if the signals in both channels are identical or heavily correlated, the angle residue becomes zero or minimal, allowing the residue encoder to quantize the data with minimal bit consumption.

The Role of the Floor Matrix

The "floor matrix" represents the channel-to-floor assignment scheme configured in the Vorbis submap settings. Vorbis does not force coupled residue channels to share the same floor envelope. Instead:

  1. Independent Envelope Extraction: The encoder computes separate floor curves for the left and right channels (\(Floor_L\) and \(Floor_R\)). Each floor curve reflects the exact masking threshold and absolute energy distribution of its respective channel.
  2. Residue Normalization: Both channel spectra are divided by their individual floors prior to coupling: \[Residue_L = \frac{Spectrum_L}{Floor_L}\] \[Residue_R = \frac{Spectrum_R}{Floor_R}\]
  3. Polar Decoupling: The decoder reads the coupled residue vectors and resolves the magnitude and angle components back into discrete normalized channel residues (\(Residue_L'\) and \(Residue_R'\)).
  4. Rescaling via the Floor: The decoder synthesizes the final MDCT spectrum for each channel by multiplying the decoupled residue vectors by their corresponding floor envelopes: \[Spectrum_L = Residue_L' \times Floor_L\] \[Spectrum_R = Residue_R' \times Floor_R\]

Why This Method Prevents Stereo Image Collapse

In conventional joint stereo schemes (such as MPEG-1 Layer III joint stereo), coupling can lead to "panning bleed" or loss of high-frequency spatial cues when phase relationships are imperfect.

Vorbis eliminates this problem through the division between floor and residue. Even when high-frequency residue data is heavily quantized or completely shared across channels through coupling, the independent floor curves preserve the exact volume and psychoacoustic balance for each channel. The spatial placement of an instrument or sound source is determined largely by interaural level differences encoded in the floor matrix, allowing Vorbis to preserve stereo imaging accurately even at low bitrates.
