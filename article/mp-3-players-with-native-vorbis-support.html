MP3 Players With Native Vorbis Support

During the peak of the digital audio player era in the 2000s, several major commercial hardware manufacturers offered native, factory-installed support for the open-source Ogg Vorbis audio format. While the market was dominated by proprietary standards such as MP3 and WMA, brands catering to audiophiles and open-source enthusiasts licensed DSP architectures capable of decoding Vorbis directly out of the box without requiring third-party firmware like Rockbox.

Rio Karma

Released in 2003 by Rio (then owned by Digital Networks North America), the Rio Karma was one of the earliest and most notable commercial hard-drive players to feature native Ogg Vorbis decoding directly from the factory. In addition to Vorbis, it supported FLAC and gapless playback, making it an immediate favorite among audiophiles looking for high-fidelity, open-source audio compression on the go.

Cowon (iAUDIO)

South Korean electronics manufacturer Cowon made multi-format playback a core selling point of its iAUDIO lineup. Beginning in the early 2000s with flash-based devices like the iAUDIO CW300, 4, U2, and G3, Cowon included native Ogg Vorbis playback out of the box. Later hard-drive and multimedia models, such as the Cowon X5 and the Cowon D2, continued this trend by handling Vorbis files up to the highest quality settings (Q10) across wide bitrates.

iRiver

iRiver was another prominent manufacturer that embraced the format. Although some early models required official firmware updates to enable it, subsequent models shipped with native Vorbis support pre-installed. The iFP flash player series (including the iFP-700 and iFP-800 series) and the flagship hard-drive players—the H100 and H300 series (originally sold as the iHP-120 and iHP-320)—handled Ogg Vorbis natively alongside MP3 and WMA.

SanDisk Sansa Series

SanDisk integrated Vorbis into some of the most mainstream, mass-market budget players in North America and Europe. While early firmware versions of the Sansa e200 series lacked it, later revisions and subsequent lines—most notably the Sansa Clip, Clip+, Clip Zip, and Sansa Fuze—included native Ogg Vorbis and FLAC decoding straight from the factory. These tiny flash players became wildly popular because of their low price point and comprehensive codec support.

TrekStor and Meizu

Other international manufacturers also adopted the format natively:

  • Meizu: The Chinese manufacturer provided native Ogg Vorbis playback on its popular mid-2000s portable media players, including the Meizu M6 Mini Player and Meizu M3 Music Card.
  • TrekStor: Several devices in the German manufacturer's Vibez and i.Beat lines supported Ogg Vorbis from the factory, marketing open-codec flexibility to European consumers.

Why Native Vorbis Was Not Universal

While Vorbis was technically superior to MP3 in audio quality per bitrate and free from patent licensing fees, commercial adoption remained limited to select brands. Most consumer chipsets were hardwired with application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) optimized strictly for MP3 and WMA. Decoding Vorbis required more computationally intensive programmable Digital Signal Processors (DSPs) and more RAM, which increased hardware costs and led to higher battery consumption. Consequently, market leaders like Apple and Sony opted out, leaving native Vorbis support primarily to brands targeting enthusiast demographics.
