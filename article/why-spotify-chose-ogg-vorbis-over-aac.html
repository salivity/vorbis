Why Spotify Chose Ogg Vorbis Over AAC

When Spotify launched in 2008, it faced the dual challenge of delivering low-latency, high-fidelity audio streams while managing strict startup operating costs. The decision to use the open-source Ogg Vorbis format rather than the industry-standard Advanced Audio Coding (AAC) format stemmed from licensing constraints, platform portability, competitive compression quality, and the requirements of Spotify’s proprietary early peer-to-peer (P2P) distribution network.

Zero Licensing Fees and Patent Freedom

The primary factor driving Spotify’s adoption of Vorbis was financial and legal overhead. AAC is encumbered by extensive patent pools managed by organizations such as Via Licensing and MPEG LA. Deploying AAC at scale required paying per-unit distribution royalties and software decoder fees, which represented a severe financial barrier for a young European startup operating on venture capital.

In contrast, Ogg Vorbis—developed by the Xiph.Org Foundation—is an open-source, patent-free, and royalty-free specification. By selecting Vorbis, Spotify eliminated recurring streaming codec licensing fees and retained full legal freedom to deploy custom client and server implementations without complex commercial audits.

Alignment with Early P2P Architecture

During its initial years, Spotify kept content delivery network (CDN) bandwidth costs low by utilizing a bespoke peer-to-peer network on desktop clients. Connected desktop users acted as network nodes, caching and uploading chunks of audio tracks to neighboring listeners to ensure sub-millisecond playback initiation.

Distributing proprietary formats like AAC across a decentralized, multi-hop peer network introduced legal gray areas and potential licensing disputes regarding unauthorized redistribution rights. Vorbis was unrestricted, making it legally risk-free to slice into chunks, distribute across peers, and reassemble on the receiving end.

Cross-Platform Implementation and Portability

Spotify originally launched as a standalone desktop application for Windows and macOS, with native Linux clients arriving shortly after. Native system-level decoding for AAC was inconsistent across these operating systems in the late 2000s, often forcing developers to bundle proprietary decoding software.

Vorbis provided mature, lightweight, open-source reference implementations, notably libvorbis and the fixed-point decoder Tremor. Because Spotify possessed the complete source code, their engineering team could easily embed, optimize, and customize the decoder across all target desktop platforms without third-party dependencies or commercial software development kit (SDK) agreements.

Competitive Audio Performance at Target Bitrates

Spotify initially deployed streams at three primary tiers: 96 kbps for bandwidth-constrained scenarios, 160 kbps for standard desktop playback, and 320 kbps for premium users. While AAC-LC (and later HE-AAC) offered high efficiency, Vorbis matched or exceeded AAC's perceived acoustic quality at the standard 160 kbps and 320 kbps bitrates. Given that Vorbis provided near-identical transparency to AAC without the associated costs, there was no perceptual audio incentive to choose AAC over its open-source alternative.
