Vorbis Residue 0, Residue 1, and Residue 2 Differences

In the Vorbis audio compression format, the residue encoding stage is responsible for reconstructing the detailed spectral residue vectors after the spectral floor curve has been subtracted. The primary difference between Residue 0, Residue 1, and Residue 2 lies in how multi-channel audio data is organized, interleaved, and mapped to vector quantization codebooks during decoding. While Residue 0 handles channels independently, Residues 1 and 2 interleave channel data to exploit inter-channel redundancies, using different geometric alignments for their codebook vectors.

The Role of Residue in Vorbis

During Vorbis encoding, audio is transformed into the frequency domain via a Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT). The gross spectral envelope is modeled as the "floor," and the remaining fine frequency detail is the "residue." Residue vectors are quantized into multidimensional packets using predefined codebooks across multiple passes. The decoder reads these residue types to place reconstructed MDCT coefficients back into their respective channel buffers.

Residue 0: Independent Channel Encoding

Residue 0 processes each audio channel completely independently.

  • Structure: The spectral coefficients of each channel are treated as separate, self-contained linear vectors.
  • Decoding Behavior: The decoder finishes reconstructing an entire residue vector for Channel 0 before moving to Channel 1, Channel 2, and so on. A single codebook lookup of dimension \(N\) supplies \(N\) consecutive spectral bins to the active channel.
  • Use Case: Residue 0 is simplest in terms of memory layout, but it cannot exploit correlation between channels. It is primarily used for true mono streams or multi-channel material where channels share little to no phase or amplitude correlation.

Residue 1: Interleaved Sequence Encoding

Residue 1 introduces multi-channel awareness by interleaving the spectral bins of all channels into a single unified array before applying vector quantization.

  • Structure: If an audio stream has two channels (\(L\) and \(R\)), the residue sequence is arranged alternately: \([L_0, R_0, L_1, R_1, L_2, R_2, \dots]\).
  • Decoding Behavior: The codebook decodes vectors sequentially through this interleaved array. If a codebook entry has a dimension of \(N\), those \(N\) values are written sequentially across the interleaved stream. Depending on the codebook dimension and channel count, a single codebook vector might cross channel boundaries or populate consecutive frequency bins across different channels.
  • Use Case: Residue 1 provides compression gains over Residue 0 when channels share statistical energy properties, allowing a single codebook model to efficiently cover multi-channel dynamics.

Residue 2: Parallel Inter-Channel Encoding

Residue 2 also interleaves channels, but it explicitly aligns codebook vector dimensions directly across channels at identical frequency locations.

  • Structure: The channels are interleaved such that codebook lookups decode values for all channels at the exact same spectral bin simultaneously.
  • Decoding Behavior: In a stereo stream (\(L\) and \(R\)) using a 2-dimensional codebook, a single codebook entry produces a pair of coefficients: value 0 maps to \(L_k\) and value 1 maps to \(R_k\). The decoder steps frequency-by-frequency, populating equivalent positions in all channels simultaneously.
  • Use Case: Residue 2 is the standard choice for stereo and coupled multi-channel Vorbis streams. Because left and right audio channels often have highly correlated frequency content, grouping \((L_k, R_k)\) into a single multidimensional vector allows the encoder to exploit spatial redundancy directly, which is critical for Vorbis's channel-coupling (joint stereo) implementation.
