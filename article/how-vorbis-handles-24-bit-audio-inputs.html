How Vorbis Handles 24-Bit Audio Inputs

The Ogg Vorbis audio compression format natively accommodates 24-bit audio inputs by converting raw integer samples into an internal floating-point representation. Unlike legacy lossy codecs that downsample or truncate audio to 16-bit integer depths prior to processing, Vorbis preserves the extended dynamic range of high-resolution sources throughout its analysis phase. This article explains the technical mechanics behind Vorbis’s handling of 24-bit PCM inputs, including floating-point normalization, psychoacoustic quantization, frequency management, and decoding considerations.

Native Floating-Point Architecture

The core Vorbis encoding library, libvorbis, does not process audio as integer data. When a 24-bit linear PCM input is fed into the encoder, the samples are directly normalized into a 32-bit floating-point format, typically scaled within the range of -1.0 to +1.0.

Because standard single-precision 32-bit floating-point numbers provide 24 bits of significand (mantissa) precision, the full dynamic range of a 24-bit integer source (approximately 144 dB) is mapped into the encoder without immediate precision loss or truncation. Consequently, there is no need to apply dither or reduce the dynamic range to 16-bit (96 dB) before the encoder begins its psychoacoustic analysis.

Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) and Quantization

Once the 24-bit samples are in the floating-point domain, Vorbis applies windowing and converts time-domain signals into frequency-domain representations via the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT).

At this stage, the concept of a strict "bit depth" ceases to apply in the traditional PCM sense. Vorbis is a lossy, perceptual codec that discards spectral information deemed inaudible based on human psychoacoustic masking models:

  • Spectral Floor: Vorbis models the overall spectral shape (the "floor") using either line spectral pairs (LSP) or piecewise linear representation. The high dynamic range of the 24-bit input allows the encoder to characterize low-level details in quiet passages more accurately.
  • Residue Encoding: The differences between the spectral floor and the actual audio signal (the "residue") are quantized using vector quantization codebooks.

While the source dynamic range is preserved initially, the lossy nature of Vorbis means that high-frequency, low-amplitude details may be discarded or simplified depending on the selected quality level (-q setting).

Sample Rates and Low-Pass Filtering

High-resolution 24-bit audio files often feature high sampling rates, such as 88.2 kHz, 96 kHz, or 192 kHz. Vorbis fully supports arbitrary sample rates up to several hundred kilohertz.

However, standard Vorbis tuning utilizes low-pass filters by default to optimize bit allocation for human hearing. Unless explicitly configured with custom parameters or very high quality thresholds (e.g., -q 9 or -q 10), the encoder may filter out ultrasonic frequencies above 20 kHz to 24 kHz. The 24-bit dynamic range is retained for audible frequencies, but ultrasonic bandwidth is intentionally discarded to prevent wasting bitrate on inaudible components.

Decoding and Reconstruction

When an Ogg Vorbis stream is decoded, the output is reconstructed from the frequency domain back into floating-point PCM. Modern decoders (libvorbisfile or tremor) can deliver this output directly as 32-bit floating-point samples or quantize it into 24-bit or 32-bit integer PCM buffers.

Because decoding occurs in the floating-point domain, reconstructing to 24-bit output avoids the quantization noise that would occur if the stream were forced through a 16-bit intermediate bottleneck. This ensures that the low noise floor of the original 24-bit recording remains intact to the highest extent allowed by the lossy compression algorithm.
