Ogg Vorbis Bitrate During Complete Silence

During complete digital silence, an Ogg Vorbis audio stream typically drops to a baseline bitrate between 1.2 kbps and 2.5 kbps. Because Vorbis utilizes a native variable bitrate (VBR) architecture, it aggressively reduces data consumption when processing silent audio, but it does not drop to absolute zero. This remaining bandwidth is consumed almost entirely by required frame headers, floor definitions, and Ogg container encapsulation rather than actual acoustic data.

Why Vorbis Cannot Drop to 0 kbps

Unlike speech-oriented codecs such as Opus or Speex, Vorbis lacks a native Discontinuous Transmission (DTX) mode. In an audio codec using DTX, the encoder can pause transmission entirely during silence and instruct the decoder to generate comfort noise or nothing at all.

Vorbis, designed as a general-purpose audio codec, requires continuous packet delivery. It analyzes audio in overlapping time-domain blocks (typically switching between 256 and 2048 samples at standard sample rates like 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz). Because the decoder must continually reconstruct overlapping windowed segments to prevent discontinuities, the encoder must transmit a packet for every single audio frame, even when no sound energy exists.

Packet Size During Silence

When encoding absolute digital silence (all PCM samples at zero):

  • Residue: The residual audio spectrum contains no energy, meaning zero bits are required to encode acoustic details.
  • Floor: The spectral floor is encoded at the minimum possible state using minimal codebook indices, requiring only a handful of bits.
  • Headers: Vorbis packet headers specify the window type and framing information.

Under these conditions, an individual raw Vorbis packet encoding 2048 samples of pure silence can be as small as 2 to 4 bytes.

Container and Protocol Overhead

At 44.1 kHz using long 2048-sample windows, the encoder outputs approximately 21.5 packets per second. Multiplying this packet rate by minimal packet sizes results in raw Vorbis stream rates often below 1 kbps.

However, Vorbis audio is typically wrapped inside the Ogg transport container. The Ogg layer groups packets into pages, adding an obligatory 27-byte header per page along with segment tables. Depending on encoder tuning—specifically how often Ogg pages are flushed—the container overhead pushes the observed stream bitrate up to the standard range of 1.4 kbps to 2.5 kbps.

Consequently, listeners streaming or downloading an Ogg Vorbis file across prolonged periods of absolute silence will observe bandwidth usage stabilizing at roughly 1 to 2 kilobits per second.
