How Vorbis Groups Spectral Lines for Quantization

The Vorbis audio codec compresses audio by converting time-domain PCM samples into frequency-domain spectral lines via the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT), then grouping those lines into critical sub-bands tailored to human psychoacoustics. Rather than applying a uniform quantization grid across the entire frequency range, Vorbis separates the spectrum into a spectral envelope ("floor") and remaining harmonic details ("residue"). By grouping MDCT spectral lines into non-uniform partitions based on critical bands—modeled after the Bark scale—Vorbis allocates bit budgets according to human auditory masking thresholds, concentrating precision where the ear is most sensitive.

Time-to-Frequency Transformation

The encoding process begins by dividing the input audio signal into overlapping windows and transforming them via the MDCT. This yields a set of discrete frequency coefficients, commonly referred to as spectral lines or MDCT bins. Because human hearing does not perceive frequency differences linearly, these raw spectral lines cannot be quantized uniformly without introducing perceptible distortion or wasting bits.

The Bark Scale and Auditory Masking

Vorbis groups spectral lines using psychoacoustic principles derived from critical bands. In human hearing, frequencies within a single critical band mask weaker adjacent sounds. Vorbis translates linear MDCT frequencies (\(f\)) into the Bark scale (\(z\)), typically approximating it with equations such as:

\[z = 13.1 \arctan(0.00076 f) + 3.5 \arctan\left(\left(\frac{f}{7500}\right)^2\right)\]

In this perceptual domain, critical bands have roughly equal widths. Consequently, MDCT spectral lines corresponding to lower frequencies are grouped into narrow sub-bands containing few lines, while higher-frequency sub-bands encompass substantially wider spans of spectral lines.

Separation of Floor and Residue

Vorbis handles spectral quantization by decoupling the signal into two distinct components:

  1. Floor (Spectral Envelope): A macro-level curve approximating the overall energy and masking threshold across the frequency spectrum.
  2. Residue (Fine Structure): The raw spectral lines divided by the floor curve, representing the normalized fine-grain harmonics and noise.

Both components rely on critical band grouping, but they execute it through different mechanisms.

Floor Grouping: Floor 0 vs. Floor 1

Vorbis specifies two floor mechanisms that govern how spectral lines are gathered into critical bands:

  • Floor 0 (LSP/LCP Model): Floor 0 computes a spectral envelope using Linear Predictive Coding (LPC) and converts it to Line Spectral Pairs (LSP). The synthesis filter generates a continuous curve warped directly to the Bark scale. In this mode, sub-band grouping is continuous rather than discrete, mapping spectral lines dynamically along the Bark-warped axis.
  • Floor 1 (Piecewise Linear Model): Modern Vorbis encoders primarily use Floor 1. It establishes a set of fixed frequency points (nodes) along the spectrum. These nodes are placed closer together at lower frequencies and farther apart at higher frequencies, mirroring critical bandwidths. The encoder evaluates the MDCT lines between these nodes, computes an amplitude value for each node, and interpolates linearly (in the logarithmic domain) between them to establish the masking envelope.

Residue Partitioning and Quantization

Once the floor is established, the original MDCT spectral lines are divided by the synthesized floor curve. The resulting normalized coefficients are grouped into residue partitions for vector quantization (VQ).

  1. Sub-band Partitions: The full range of MDCT lines is split into contiguous, non-overlapping blocks called partitions. Low-frequency partitions span fewer lines to preserve spectral resolution, whereas high-frequency partitions span broader arrays of lines.
  2. Classification: Each partition is assigned a "class" based on its energy content. Silence or heavily masked partitions receive low-energy class designations, while prominent tonalities receive higher classes.
  3. Vector Quantization (VQ): Within each partition, the spectral lines are grouped into small multi-dimensional vectors (commonly 2, 4, or 8 adjacent lines). These vectors are matched against predetermined multidimensional codebooks using cascaded passes.
  4. Entropy Coding: Partitions with similar statistical characteristics share Huffman codebooks, encoding multiple spectral lines per symbol to maximize compression efficiency.

By decoupling the psychoacoustic envelope (Floor) from the underlying spectral details (Residue) and partitioning both according to critical bandwidths, Vorbis groups MDCT spectral lines efficiently, suppressing quantization noise below the human threshold of hearing.
