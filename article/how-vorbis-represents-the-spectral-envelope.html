How Vorbis Represents the Spectral Envelope

The Vorbis audio codec represents the spectral envelope of an audio frame through a component known as the "floor." In the Vorbis encoding model, the frequency spectrum generated by the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) is decomposed into two distinct layers: the spectral envelope (floor), which captures the broad, low-resolution energy distribution across frequencies, and the fine spectral structure (residue), which captures the remaining harmonic details. This article explains the architectural separation between floor and residue, the two mechanisms Vorbis uses to encode the floor curve (Floor 0 and Floor 1), and how the decoder reconstructs the continuous envelope to synthesize audio.

The Floor and Residue Architecture

Vorbis separates psychoacoustic masking curves from individual spectral lines. Instead of quantizing individual MDCT frequency bins directly, Vorbis models the overall shape of the spectrum using a floor curve.

Once the encoder determines the floor curve, it divides the original MDCT spectrum by this envelope to produce a normalized "residue." The floor represents the perceptual masking threshold and broad energy contours, allowing the residue to be quantized more uniformly and efficiently with vector quantization.

Floor 1: Piecewise-Linear Representation

Floor 1 is the primary and most commonly used method for encoding the spectral envelope in Vorbis streams. It represents the spectral envelope as a series of connected line segments on a logarithmic frequency scale (approximating the psychoacoustic Bark scale) and a decibel amplitude scale.

  • Anchor Points: The curve is defined by a set of frequency-amplitude coordinate pairs \((X, Y)\). The frequency values (\(X\)) are predetermined and fixed for a given frame configuration, arranged monotonically from zero to the Nyquist frequency. The amplitude values (\(Y\)) are quantized integers representing signal energy in decibels.
  • Differential Coding: To save bits, the amplitudes (\(Y\) values) are not stored as raw absolute numbers. Instead, Vorbis encodes the two boundary endpoints first, then recursively encodes intermediate points as deltas relative to values predicted by linear interpolation of already-decoded neighbor points.
  • Synthesis via Linear Interpolation: During decoding, once the \((X, Y)\) nodes are reconstructed, the decoder draws straight lines between adjacent points in the log-frequency/dB domain. This creates a continuous, piecewise-linear envelope that spans the entire frequency spectrum of the frame.

Floor 1 offers robust, predictable behavior without algorithmic instability, making it the standard choice in practical Vorbis implementations.

Floor 0: Linear Predictive Coding (LPC)

Vorbis also includes an alternative envelope mechanism called Floor 0. Rather than using discrete anchor points, Floor 0 represents the spectral envelope using Linear Predictive Coding (LPC) techniques:

  • LPC to LSF Conversion: The encoder computes an LPC filter that approximates the spectral envelope and converts the filter coefficients into Line Spectral Frequencies (LSF), also referred to as Line Spectral Pairs (LSP).
  • Quantization: The LSFs are quantized using multi-stage vector quantization, taking advantage of the ordered properties of spectral pairs to maintain filter stability.
  • Curve Generation: At the decoder, the LSF parameters are mapped onto a Bark-scale frequency grid to synthesize a smooth curve that mimics the frequency response of the LPC synthesis filter.

While Floor 0 was capable of producing smooth curves with low bit counts, it was largely phased out in favor of Floor 1 due to the computational complexity of the LSF-to-curve generation and the superior localized control offered by Floor 1's piecewise nodes.

Final Reconstruction

Regardless of whether Floor 0 or Floor 1 is used, the decoding output is a vector of amplitude values matching the length of the frame's MDCT spectrum. The decoder multiplies this decoded spectral envelope element-by-element with the decoded residue vector. This scales the normalized residue back to its original dynamic range, completing the reconstruction of the frequency spectrum prior to running the inverse MDCT.
