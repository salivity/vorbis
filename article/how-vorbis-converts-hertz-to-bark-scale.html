How Vorbis Converts Hertz to Bark Scale

This article explains how the Vorbis audio codec maps linear frequency values measured in Hertz (Hz) to the non-linear, perceptually motivated Bark scale. It breaks down the mathematical formula Vorbis implements in its source code, examines why this conversion is necessary for lossy audio compression, and details how the specific terms in the equation shape frequency resolution across human hearing ranges.

The Role of the Bark Scale in Vorbis

The human ear processes sound non-linearly, resolving individual frequency differences much more effectively at lower frequencies than at higher ones. Psychoacoustic research groups these hearing zones into "critical bands," which the Bark scale standardizes from 1 to roughly 24 Bark.

Vorbis uses the Bark scale—primarily in its Floor 0 envelope representation and psychoacoustic masking models—to allocate bits efficiently. By transforming linear frequencies (Hertz) into Bark values, Vorbis creates an envelope space where spectral energy distribution mirrors how humans perceive loudness and masking thresholds.

The Vorbis Conversion Formula

Vorbis implements a specific approximation formula to convert a frequency \(f\) (in Hz) to a Bark value \(b\). Defined in the reference implementation header scales.h as toBARK(n), the mathematical function is:

\[b(f) = 13.1 \cdot \arctan(0.00074 \cdot f) + 2.24 \cdot \arctan(f^2 \cdot 1.85 \times 10^{-8}) + 10^{-4} \cdot f\]

Where:

  • \(f\) is the input frequency in Hertz.
  • \(\arctan\) produces results in radians.
  • \(b(f)\) is the resulting position on the Bark scale.

Mathematical Breakdown of the Function

The formula is a custom three-part curve-fit designed to approximate empirical critical-band data smoothly across the entire audible spectrum (0 Hz to over 20 kHz):

  1. First Arctangent Term (\(13.1 \cdot \arctan(0.00074 \cdot f)\)):
    This term governs the lower-to-middle frequency ranges (up to roughly 2 kHz). For low values of \(f\), \(\arctan(x) \approx x\), producing a roughly linear mapping below 500 Hz where critical bandwidth is relatively constant. As frequency increases, the arctangent saturates, providing the transition into logarithmic behavior.

  2. Second Arctangent Term (\(2.24 \cdot \arctan(f^2 \cdot 1.85 \times 10^{-8})\)):
    This term accounts for higher-frequency dispersion. Because the input frequency is squared (\(f^2\)), it remains negligible at low and middle frequencies but accelerates significantly above 4 kHz before saturating, matching the widening of critical bandwidths in upper registers.

  3. Linear Offset Term (\(10^{-4} \cdot f\)):
    The small linear factor ensures that the curve continues to rise monotonically, preventing the combined arctangent terms from completely flattening out at extreme high frequencies near or beyond the Nyquist limit.

Implementation in the Codec Pipeline

During encoding, spectral coefficients obtained via the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) represent equal frequency steps in Hertz. Vorbis maps each spectral bin to its corresponding Bark value using the conversion formula.

This transformation maps the dense, high-frequency bins into wider Bark bands, while lower-frequency bins remain finely partitioned. The encoder then computes masking thresholds along this uniform Bark axis, ensuring quantization noise is shaped to sit just below human audibility limits without wasting bitrate on inaudible high-frequency fidelity.
