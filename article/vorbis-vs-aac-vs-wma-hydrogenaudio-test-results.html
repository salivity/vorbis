Vorbis vs AAC vs WMA Hydrogenaudio Test Results

During the 2000s, the Hydrogenaudio community conducted a series of rigorous, double-blind listening tests to objectively evaluate the performance of modern lossy audio codecs, primarily focusing on Ogg Vorbis, AAC, and WMA alongside MP3. These controlled community trials demonstrated that Vorbis was a top-tier performer, frequently matching or exceeding commercial AAC implementations at mid-range bitrates and consistently outperforming standard WMA codecs across multiple testing rounds.

Hydrogenaudio relied on double-blind ABX and MUSHRA (MPEG Audio Subjective Assessment) methodologies using software tools like ABC/HR. By stripping away branding and file metadata, listeners evaluated audio samples without confirmation bias. Test samples included both mainstream music and known "killer samples"—tracks containing sharp transients, castanets, harpsichords, and spoken vocals designed to stress psychoacoustic algorithms.

In the mid-bitrate tier, typically centered around 128 kbps, community tests consistently ranked Vorbis and AAC as superior to older codecs like MP3 and Microsoft's standard WMA encoder. Listeners found that Vorbis and high-grade AAC implementations (such as Nero Digital AAC and Apple QuickTime AAC) reached near-transparent quality on standard stereo audio. In contrast, standard WMA frequently exhibited metallic artifacts, low-pass filtering, and stereo-imaging collapse at 128 kbps, trailing behind Vorbis and AAC in statistical significance.

At lower bitrates, specifically around 64 kbps to 96 kbps, the evaluation split based on the profiles used. While High-Efficiency AAC (HE-AAC/aacPlus) dominated the lowest brackets (sub-64 kbps) due to its use of Spectral Band Replication, Vorbis proved exceptionally competitive against standard Low Complexity AAC (LC-AAC) and WMA Standard at 80 to 96 kbps. WMA typically suffered from audible muffled highs or flanging artifacts at these lower targets, whereas Vorbis preserved a broader, more natural acoustic bandwidth.

A defining factor in these results was the development of the "aoTuV" tunings for the Vorbis encoder. Initiated by community contributor Aoyumi, these third-party algorithmic improvements were extensively validated through Hydrogenaudio's blind tests. The aoTuV tunings drastically resolved Vorbis's early struggles with pre-echo and high-frequency ringing, propelling open-source Vorbis into direct parity with the industry's best proprietary AAC encoders and firmly ahead of WMA.
