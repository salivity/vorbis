How Vorbis Evaluates Harmonic and Inharmonic Sounds

The Ogg Vorbis audio codec relies on an advanced psychoacoustic model to optimize compression by differentiating between harmonic (tonal) and inharmonic (noise-like) sound components. Because the human auditory system perceives distortion differently depending on whether a sound is a pure tone or diffuse noise, Vorbis evaluates the spectral structure of an audio frame to dynamically adjust its masking thresholds. This article explores the mathematical and perceptual methods Vorbis uses to classify these sound characteristics, calculate masking curves, and allocate bits efficiently.

Spectral Decomposition via the MDCT

Vorbis begins its evaluation by transforming time-domain audio samples into the frequency domain using the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT). This process uses overlapping windows—typically switching between short windows (to mitigate pre-echo during transients) and long windows (to maximize spectral resolution for stationary signals). Once in the frequency domain, the spectrum reveals distinct energy distributions: harmonic sounds appear as sharp, isolated spectral peaks, whereas inharmonic sounds manifest as distributed, flat, or chaotic energy across wider frequency bands.

Tonality Estimation and Peak Detection

To separate harmonic components from inharmonic noise, Vorbis calculates the "tonality" of the spectral bins. It evaluates the spectral envelope and local energy distribution through several metrics:

  • Spectral Flatness Measure (SFM): By comparing the geometric mean to the arithmetic mean of spectral coefficients within a given critical band, Vorbis determines how peaky or flat the spectrum is. A low flatness score indicates a prominent harmonic peak, whereas a high score indicates noise-like, inharmonic energy.
  • Peak-to-Average Ratio: Vorbis scans for prominent local maxima relative to surrounding frequency bins. Sharp peaks surrounded by lower-energy valleys are cataloged as tonal candidates.
  • Temporal Predictability: Harmonic signals tend to exhibit predictable phase and magnitude progression across successive analysis windows. Inharmonic or noise-like components show rapid, uncorrelated variations from frame to frame.

Asymmetry in Psychoacoustic Masking

The distinction between harmonic and inharmonic energy is critical because the human ear exhibits asymmetric masking properties:

  1. Tone-Masking-Noise (TMN): When a strong harmonic tone masks broadband noise, the masking threshold is relatively low. Human hearing easily identifies "hiss" or distortion around a pure tone, meaning the allowable quantization noise must be kept strictly below the tone's level.
  2. Noise-Masking-Tone (NMT): When broadband or inharmonic noise masks a pure tone, the masking threshold is much higher. The auditory system struggles to detect added tones or distortion within complex, noisy textures.

Vorbis uses an energy-dependent tonality index ranging from pure tone to pure noise. As the evaluated segment leans toward harmonicity, the codec lowers the allowed noise ceiling. When the segment is determined to be inharmonic, the threshold is elevated, permitting heavier quantization without audible artifacts.

The Floor 1 Representation

Once Vorbis determines the masking contribution of harmonic peaks and inharmonic noise fields, it synthesizes these values into a global masking curve. In Vorbis, this spectral envelope is primarily encoded using the Floor 1 mechanism.

Floor 1 fits a piecewise linear curve to the logarithmic frequency scale (modeled closely after the Bark scale). The control points of this curve are directly driven by the harmonic evaluation:

  • At harmonic frequencies, control points are tied closely to the peak energy, ensuring the floor does not introduce excessive quantization error that would compromise tonal clarity.
  • In inharmonic or noise-dominated regions, control points smooth out, bridging wider bands to minimize the bits required to describe the floor envelope itself.

Residue Vector Quantization

After defining the floor curve, Vorbis divides the original MDCT spectrum by this envelope to produce a normalized "residue." Because the harmonic versus inharmonic evaluation already shaped the floor to reflect human masking limits, the residue represents only the perceptually significant details. Inharmonic regions with high masking allowances result in flattened residues that require fewer bits to encode, while harmonic regions retain precision where the human ear is most sensitive to pitch and timbre.
