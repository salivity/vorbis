Vorbis Floor 1 Piecewise Linear Interpolation

In the Vorbis audio codec, the Floor 1 subsystem defines the broad spectral envelope of an audio frame by approximating energy distribution across frequencies using piecewise linear interpolation. This article breaks down how Floor 1 defines coordinate nodes, hierarchically encodes amplitude values, and applies deterministic, integer-based linear rendering in the decibel domain to reconstruct an accurate psychoacoustic masking curve.

The Role of the Spectral Floor

Vorbis separates audio spectrum representation into two distinct elements: the "floor" and the "residue." The floor represents the overall energy envelope across frequencies, modeling human auditory thresholds and masking curves. The residue captures the fine spectral details that deviate from this envelope. Floor 1 is the primary mechanism used in Vorbis to describe this envelope efficiently.

Node Configuration and the Frequency Axis (X-Values)

Floor 1 establishes a sequence of discrete frequency bins, known as nodes or control points. The horizontal positions (\(X\)-values) of these nodes are fixed during stream initialization and transmitted in the Vorbis setup header:

  • Non-Uniform Spacing: The \(X\)-coordinates are arranged non-linearly across the spectrum, spaced more densely at lower frequencies and farther apart at higher frequencies. This mirrors the Bark scale or equivalent rectangular bandwidth (ERB) curves of human hearing.
  • Range Bounds: Every Floor 1 configuration includes two mandatory endpoint nodes representing the lowest and highest frequency points (bin 0 and the Nyquist limit).

Because the encoder and decoder share the exact same predefined \(X\)-coordinates, the audio stream only needs to transmit the amplitude values (\(Y\)-values) for each active node per audio frame.

Hierarchical Amplitude Encoding (Y-Values)

Rather than storing the \(Y\)-values independently, Floor 1 uses a hierarchical tree structure to predict amplitudes:

  1. Endpoint Quantization: The two edge nodes are quantized and encoded first.
  2. Intermediate Prediction: For any node situated between two already established nodes, its predicted value is calculated via linear interpolation between those two surrounding anchor points.
  3. Residual Transmission: The encoder calculates the difference between the node’s actual energy value and its linearly predicted value. This difference is Huffman-coded. If a node does not deviate meaningfully from the linear path, its residual can be omitted entirely.

Piecewise Linear Interpolation Mechanics

Once the decoder recovers all node values \((X_i, Y_i)\), it connects adjacent nodes with straight line segments across the frequency spectrum.

1. Interpolation in the Decibel Domain

The \(Y\)-coordinates represent signal magnitude on a logarithmic (decibel) scale. Performing standard linear interpolation between two points \((X_A, Y_A)\) and \((X_B, Y_B)\) yields intermediate values along a straight line in the dB domain:

\[Y(x) = Y_A + (Y_B - Y_A) \cdot \frac{x - X_A}{X_B - X_A}\]

Because the vertical axis is logarithmic, a straight line connecting two nodes translates to an exponential curve when converted back to linear amplitude. This directly matches human perception of sound loudness across frequency intervals.

2. Deterministic Integer Arithmetic

Digital audio decoders running on different hardware architectures often produce slight variations in floating-point calculations. To prevent drift and ensure identical output across platforms, the Vorbis specification mandates a bit-exact, integer-only line drawing algorithm derived from Bresenham’s algorithm.

The specification explicitly dictates how integer division, truncation, and rounding must occur when stepping from \(X_A\) to \(X_B\). This ensures that every compliant Vorbis decoder constructs the exact same sequence of discrete integer floor values across all MDCT (Modified Discrete Cosine Transform) bins.

Application to the Audio Spectrum

After the piecewise line segments are generated across the entire spectrum, the resulting curve represents the completed spectral floor:

  1. The integer floor values are mapped back to linear scale amplitudes via a lookup table or de-quantization formula.
  2. The decoder reads the quantized MDCT residue vector from the bitstream.
  3. The residue vector is multiplied point-by-point with the interpolated linear floor curve, restoring the proper energy proportions across all frequency bands prior to the inverse MDCT transform.
