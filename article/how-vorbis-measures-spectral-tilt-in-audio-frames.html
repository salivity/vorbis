How Vorbis Measures Spectral Tilt in Audio Frames

This article explains how the Vorbis audio codec calculates and represents spectral tilt across individual audio frames. Rather than computing an isolated metric, Vorbis captures spectral tilt dynamically by decomposing each frame's Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) spectrum into a coarse spectral floor and a fine spectral residue. Through its dual-floor synthesis architectures—Floor 0 and Floor 1—the codec characterizes the global slope, energy distribution, and perceptual envelope of each frame to guide efficient bit allocation and masking.

The Floor and Residue Model

Vorbis does not treat spectral tilt as a single standalone coefficient. Instead, it handles tilt as part of the overall spectral envelope. When an audio frame is transformed into the frequency domain using the MDCT, the resulting spectrum contains both broad, low-frequency trends (the macro-envelope or tilt) and fine-grained harmonic spikes.

Vorbis separates the MDCT output into two components:

  1. The Floor Function: A low-resolution representation of the broad spectral envelope, which models global spectral energy, formant structures, and overall high-to-low frequency tilt.
  2. The Residue: The remaining fine structure and harmonics obtained by dividing the raw MDCT spectrum by the synthesized floor curve.

By modeling the spectral floor accurately, Vorbis normalizes the spectrum, effectively flattening the spectral tilt so that the remaining residue can be quantized uniformly without introducing disproportionate noise into attenuated bands.

Floor 0: LPC and Line Spectral Pairs

The Floor 0 mechanism measures spectral tilt using classic Linear Predictive Coding (LPC) techniques:

  • Autocorrelation: The encoder calculates the autocorrelation of the windowed time-domain frame or an approximation from MDCT bins.
  • Levinson-Durbin Recursion: This algorithm generates linear prediction coefficients from the autocorrelation values. The low-order polynomial fit naturally captures the macroscopic trend of the spectrum, including steep roll-offs or high-frequency emphasis (tilt).
  • Line Spectral Pairs (LSP): The LPC coefficients are converted into Line Spectral Frequencies (LSFs) or Line Spectral Pairs (LSPs) for quantization. In this domain, the distribution and clustering of LSP roots directly describe the spectral slope and formant peaks across the frame.

Because LPC minimizes mean squared error in the spectral domain, Floor 0 automatically aligns its linear filter response with the predominant tilt of the frame.

Floor 1: Piecewise Linear Bark-Scale Modeling

Floor 1 is the primary and most commonly implemented floor synthesis method in modern Vorbis encoders. It measures and encodes spectral tilt using a piecewise linear approximation:

  • Perceptual Frequency Warping: Floor 1 partitions the frame's spectrum using the Bark scale, a psychoacoustic scale that groups frequency bins into critical bands matching human hearing resolution.
  • Node Selection: Specific anchor points (nodes) are positioned across the frequency spectrum. Low frequencies contain tightly packed nodes, while higher frequencies have spaced nodes.
  • Log-Magnitude Computation: The encoder determines an energy value for each node, expressed in a logarithmic (decibel) scale.
  • Linear Interpolation: Connecting these log-amplitude nodes with straight lines yields a curve composed of discrete line segments. The slopes of these segments over wide frequency intervals explicitly define the spectral tilt across the frame.

By varying the decibel values at consecutive nodes, Floor 1 allows the encoder to tilt the synthesis curve smoothly up or down across critical acoustic boundaries.

Psychoacoustic Tilt Evaluation

During analysis, the Vorbis psychoacoustic model evaluates spectral tilt to determine masked thresholds. The encoder computes the energy distribution across short or long transform blocks:

  • Short Frames: Provide high temporal resolution for transients, measuring sudden shifts in spectral tilt caused by sharp percussive attacks.
  • Long Frames: Provide high frequency resolution, capturing subtle, steady-state spectral decay over stationary signals.

Once the tilt is represented by the floor curve, the encoder divides the linear MDCT spectrum by this curve. The resulting normalized residue has a roughly flat spectral tilt, allowing the vector quantizer to encode the remaining audio data efficiently while preventing psychoacoustic noise leaks.
