How aoTuV Eliminates Vorbis Pre-Echo on Castanets

This article examines how the aoTuV (Aoyumi's Tuned Vorbis) encoder eliminates the notorious pre-echo artifacts found in sharp acoustic transients like castanets. By detailing the failure of standard Vorbis psychoacoustic models during impulsive sounds, the following sections explain the exact mechanisms—specifically advanced transient detection, optimized window switching, and revised temporal masking curves—that aoTuV uses to maintain pristine transient fidelity.

The Castanet Problem and Pre-Echo Mechanics

Castanets represent a classic "killer sample" in lossy audio compression due to their instantaneous rise time and steep dynamic attacks. In transform codecs like Ogg Vorbis, audio is converted into the frequency domain using the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) over discrete temporal segments known as windows.

When quantization noise is introduced during the compression of an MDCT block, that noise is distributed evenly across the entire duration of the window. In a standard long block (2048 samples), if a castanet click occurs toward the end of the frame, the quantization noise spreads across the preceding silence or low-energy sound.

Human psychoacoustics features strong forward masking (where a loud sound masks quieter sounds for up to 100–200 milliseconds after it occurs), but exceptionally weak backward masking (quiet sounds are only masked for roughly 5–20 milliseconds before the transient). Because the quantization noise in a long block precedes the castanet strike by tens of milliseconds, it exceeds the auditory backward-masking threshold. The listener hears this distortion as a distinct scratching or smearing noise immediately before the click, known as pre-echo.

Aggressive Transient Detection

The reference Xiph.Org Vorbis encoder relied on conservative energy-slope metrics to detect transients. In many cases, particularly at low-to-medium bitrates, these metrics failed to recognize the micro-transients of castanets rapidly enough, causing the encoder to maintain long window modes across sharp attacks.

aoTuV directly modified the energy-flux detection algorithms. It continuously computes high-frequency differential energy between consecutive sub-frames. When high-frequency energy spikes sharply within a few samples—a hallmark of wood-on-wood impacts—aoTuV flags the event as an aggressive transient far earlier in the encoding pipeline than the standard encoder would, ensuring the transient is never caught inside an unpartitioned long transform window.

Forced Window Switching

To defeat pre-echo, MDCT codecs must switch from long windows (typically 2048 samples, optimized for frequency resolution) to short windows (typically 256 samples, optimized for time resolution).

By shrinking the window size to 256 samples (around 5.8 milliseconds at a 44.1 kHz sample rate), the temporal spread of quantization noise is physically confined to a microscopic interval. aoTuV adjusted the transition logic to immediately insert transition windows (long-to-short) followed by a burst of short windows whenever a castanet onset is detected.

Because the short block's time window is roughly 5.8 ms, any quantization noise generated by the castanet impact is strictly localized to a duration shorter than the human auditory system’s backward-masking limit (5–20 ms). Consequently, the click psychoacoustically masks its own quantization noise, making pre-echo imperceptible.

Asymmetric Noise Floor Calculations

Beyond window switching, aoTuV overhauled the psychoacoustic noise floor allocation during transient events. Standard Vorbis used an acoustic floor model that smoothed noise tolerance across the entire block.

aoTuV altered this by decoupling the noise masking thresholds across the boundary of an attack:

  1. Pre-Attack Depressurization: In the sub-frame immediately preceding the peak transient envelope, aoTuV heavily penalizes quantization error. It artificially lowers the noise threshold, forcing the encoder to allocate more bits to eliminate noise prior to the hit.
  2. Post-Attack Tolerance: aoTuV exploits natural forward temporal masking by allowing slightly higher noise floors in the milliseconds directly following the castanet hit, where the ear is naturally desensitized to high-frequency distortion.

Bit Redistribution

By tuning the Bit Reservoir and Variable Bitrate (VBR) management routines, aoTuV diverts bitrate away from stationary, easily masked background tones and channels it into transient-dense frames. When castanets strike, aoTuV briefly spikes the local bitrate to preserve both the transient spike and the preceding quiet background, completely wiping out the audible pre-echo that degraded early Vorbis implementations.
