Audio Tracks That Expose Vorbis Compression Artifacts

Evaluating the Ogg Vorbis lossy audio codec requires specific stress-testing material, often referred to in the audio engineering community as "killer samples." This guide outlines the classic reference tracks and acoustic sources known to trigger Vorbis-specific encoding flaws, explaining the precise sonic characteristics—such as sharp transients and dense high frequencies—that cause issues like pre-echo, metallic smearing, and stereo collapse.

1. Castanets (SQAM Reference Track)

The European Broadcasting Union’s Sound Quality Assessment Material (SQAM) includes an isolated castanets recording, historically recognized as one of the most brutal tests for Vorbis. Castanets produce extremely sharp, impulsive acoustic attacks against total silence.

  • The Artifact: Pre-echo. Vorbis uses a Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) that splits audio into blocks. Because the sharp rise in energy happens mid-block, quantization noise smears backward in time, creating a faint, unnatural rustling sound immediately before each strike occurs.

2. Glockenspiel and Triangle

Recordings of high-pitched, metallic percussive instruments like glockenspiels, triangles, and orchestral bells challenge the psychoacoustic bit-allocation algorithms of Vorbis.

  • The Artifact: Ringing and "chirping." The sustained, pure-tone harmonics of these instruments require substantial bit precision. When bit reservoirs are depleted, Vorbis often produces synthetic, high-frequency "birdie" or chirping noises, accompanied by ringing trails that alter the instrument's natural resonance.

3. Harpsichord Performances

Works such as Bach’s Harpsichord concertos feature rapid, bright, pluck-driven attacks paired with dense, overlapping harmonic decays.

  • The Artifact: Harmonic smearing and loss of clarity. The harpsichord's fast pluck creates transients similar to castanets, while the lingering string resonances saturate the high-frequency spectrum. Lower bitrates of Vorbis struggle to retain the airiness, often making the instrument sound dull, muffled, or buzzy.

4. Heavy Crash and Ride Cymbals

Tracks featuring sustained, aggressive drumming—such as tracks from rock or metal genres—contain high levels of uncorrelated, wideband high-frequency noise.

  • The Artifact: "Swishing" and "watery" textures. Instead of a crisp, continuous hiss of metal vibrating in air, heavily compressed Vorbis can impart a distinct watery, phased, or flanger-like modulation over cymbal decays.

5. Isolated Vocals: Suzanne Vega – "Tom's Diner"

Long utilized as an industry-standard codec testing benchmark, isolated a cappella vocals expose flaws in how a codec handles speech sibilance and breathing against quiet backgrounds.

  • The Artifact: Sibilance distortion and background coarseness. Sibilant sounds ("s", "t", and "ch") are noise-like and rapid. Vorbis can introduce subtle raspiness or gritty artifacts around consonant edges, sometimes roughening the smooth natural decay of vocal room reverberation.

6. Fast Electronic Transients and Synthesizer Sweeps

Genres like glitch, IDM, or synthpop containing dry, synthetic square-wave blips and pure pitch sweeps expose edge-case encoder behavior.

  • The Artifact: Band-edge aliasing and warbling. Sweeping frequencies reveal where Vorbis transitions between window lengths or applies low-pass filtering, resulting in audible volume dips or artificial timbral stepping across the stereo field.
