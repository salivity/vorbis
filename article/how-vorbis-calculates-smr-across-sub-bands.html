How Vorbis Calculates SMR Across Sub-Bands

The Vorbis audio codec calculates the signal-to-mask ratio (SMR) by transforming audio into the frequency domain, mapping energy levels onto a non-linear psychoacoustic scale, and determining human auditory masking thresholds across critical sub-bands. By comparing the actual signal power in a given critical band to the combined masking threshold produced by neighboring tones and noise, the encoder derives the SMR. This ratio indicates how much quantization noise can be safely introduced without being detected by human ears, directly driving the quantization of Vorbis's spectral envelope and residue vectors.

Time-to-Frequency Transformation and Bark Mapping

The process begins by converting windowed time-domain audio samples into the frequency domain using the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT). Because human auditory resolution is non-linear—higher resolution at lower frequencies and lower resolution at higher frequencies—Vorbis maps the linear MDCT frequency bins into critical bands approximating the Bark scale.

The spectrum is divided into psychoacoustic partitions or sub-bands that mirror these critical bandwidths. For each sub-band, the encoder accumulates the spectral energy, establishing the raw signal energy (\(E_{signal}\)) baseline.

Tonality and Noise Estimation

Masking effectiveness depends heavily on whether a sound is a pure tone or broad-spectrum noise. Pure tones provide less masking than noise, and noise masks noise differently than tones mask tones.

Vorbis analyzes the spectral predictability across successive MDCT frames and the local spectral tilt to assess tonality. Spectral flatness measures and temporal predictability vectors classify the energy in each sub-band on a continuum between purely tonal and purely noise-like. This classification determines the specific masking index applied during subsequent threshold calculations.

Spreading Functions and Threshold Generation

A sound at one frequency masks sounds at nearby frequencies, an effect modeled via a psychoacoustic spreading function. Vorbis calculates the masking threshold across sub-bands through the following steps:

  1. Absolute Threshold of Hearing (ATH): The codec references a baseline curve representing the quietest sound audible to the human ear in absolute silence across all frequencies.
  2. Convolution with Spreading Functions: The energy of each sub-band is convolved across adjacent Bark bands using triangular or exponential spreading curves. The spreading function slopes differently depending on direction: the downward masking slope (lower frequencies masking higher ones) is generally less steep than the upward masking slope (higher frequencies masking lower ones).
  3. Threshold Calculation: For each band \(i\), the tonal and noise-masking contributions from surrounding bands are summed along with the ATH: \[T_{mask}(i) = \max\left(\text{ATH}(i), \sum_{j} E(j) \cdot S(j, i) \cdot M_{offset}\right)\] Where \(E(j)\) is the energy of band \(j\), \(S(j, i)\) is the spreading function value from band \(j\) to \(i\), and \(M_{offset}\) is the masking offset dictated by the tonality index.

Deriving the Signal-to-Mask Ratio (SMR)

Once the masking threshold (\(T_{mask}\)) and the signal energy (\(E_{signal}\)) are calculated for each sub-band, the encoder computes the SMR in decibels:

\[\text{SMR}_{dB} = 10 \log_{10}(E_{signal}) - 10 \log_{10}(T_{mask})\]

  • A high SMR means the audio signal is significantly louder than the masking threshold, indicating that low quantization noise is permissible and more bits must be assigned to preserve fidelity.
  • A low or negative SMR means the signal is fully or partially masked by surrounding energy or the ATH, allowing aggressive quantization (or complete suppression) without causing audible distortion.

Application in the Vorbis Architecture

Unlike MP3 or AAC, which use rigid scale-factor bands for quantization directly, Vorbis uses the calculated SMR values to guide two distinct stages:

  1. The Floor Formulation: Vorbis constructs a spectral envelope (typically "Floor 1," a piecewise linear curve in the log/Bark domain) that traces the calculated masking thresholds across the sub-bands.
  2. Residue Quantization: The original MDCT spectrum is divided by the floor curve, leaving a normalized "residue." Sub-bands with high SMR receive fine vector quantization in the residue stage, while sub-bands with low SMR are quantized coarsely or grouped into low-bitrate codebooks.
