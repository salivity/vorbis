Compressing Bat Echolocation Calls with Vorbis at 96 kHz

This article evaluates whether the lossy Vorbis audio compression codec can accurately preserve bat echolocation calls recorded at a 96 kHz sampling rate. It examines the container's technical specifications, the constraints imposed by human psychoacoustic tuning, and the resulting impact on ultrasonic acoustic analysis. While Vorbis supports the technical sample rate, its inherent compression architecture makes it unsuitable for accurate bioacoustic research.

Technical Sample Rate Support vs. Psychoacoustic Design

The Ogg Vorbis specification natively supports high sampling rates, including 96 kHz and higher. A 96 kHz sample rate provides a theoretical Nyquist bandwidth limit of 48 kHz, which covers the lower ultrasonic range used by several bat species, such as noctules and big brown bats.

However, Vorbis is a perceptual, lossy audio codec engineered specifically for human listeners. Its compression model relies on human psychoacoustic principles, such as equal-loudness contours, simultaneous frequency masking, and temporal masking. The underlying algorithm assumes that frequencies above the human threshold of hearing (approximately 20 kHz) are irrelevant.

High-Frequency Low-Pass Filtering

Standard Vorbis encoders, including the reference libvorbis implementation, automatically apply low-pass filters to maximize bit-rate efficiency. Even when processing a 96 kHz stream, the encoder typically attenuates or outright discards frequency components above 20 kHz to 24 kHz.

Because bat echolocation pulses within a 96 kHz recording sit primarily between 20 kHz and 48 kHz, default Vorbis encoding strips away the vast majority of the signal. Disabling this low-pass filter requires overriding internal encoder settings, which forces the codec to allocate bits to spectral regions it was never calibrated to encode efficiently.

Waveform and Temporal Smearing

Bat echolocation pulses are characterized by extremely short, steep frequency-modulated (FM) chirps and constant-frequency (CF) components lasting only a few milliseconds. Accurate bioacoustic identification depends on sub-millisecond temporal precision and distinct harmonic structures.

Vorbis uses a modified discrete cosine transform (MDCT) with variable window sizes to encode frequency data. When processing rapid ultrasonic transients:

  • Pre-echo artifacts occur, where energy from a sharp call smears forward into the preceding silence.
  • Phase distortion alters the pulse shape, distorting measurements of call duration and inter-pulse intervals.
  • Coarse quantization in ultrasonic bands damages weak harmonics and secondary feeding-buzz components.

These alterations alter automated detection algorithms and render manual acoustic measurements scientifically invalid.

Recommended Alternatives

Because Vorbis discards and alters high-frequency information, lossy psychoacoustic formats should not be used for bat echolocation data. Researchers requiring file compression should use lossless formats instead:

  • FLAC (Free Lossless Audio Codec): Fully supports 96 kHz sampling rates and multichannel recordings while reducing file sizes by 30% to 50% without altering a single sample.
  • WavPack: Supports high-sample-rate audio with full mathematical reversibility in lossless mode.

Vorbis cannot accurately compress bat echolocation calls at 96 kHz. Although the file format can store the stream, its human-centric compression design destroys the ultrasonic signal characteristics required for species identification and acoustic research.
