How Vorbis Encodes High-Frequency Binaural Audio

This article examines how the Vorbis audio compression format processes high-frequency binaural audio recordings. It explores the psychoacoustic principles of binaural hearing, Vorbis's channel coupling architecture—specifically Mid/Side stereo and polar mapping—and how compression thresholds determine whether crucial spatial localization cues are preserved or discarded during the encoding process.

The Nature of High-Frequency Binaural Cues

Binaural recordings capture sound using two microphones placed inside an artificial or human head to mimic human hearing. Human sound localization relies on two primary mechanisms: Interaural Time Differences (ITD) for frequencies below roughly 1.5 kHz, and Interaural Level Differences (ILD) combined with Head-Related Transfer Functions (HRTFs) for frequencies above 1.5 kHz. High-frequency binaural audio relies heavily on micro-spectral notches and subtle gain differences caused by the pinnae (outer ears) to communicate vertical elevation and front-to-back positioning. Preserving these spectral subtleties is essential to maintaining an authentic three-dimensional soundstage.

Vorbis Spectral Decomposition

The Vorbis codec processes audio using the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) with dynamically switched window sizes (typically 2048 samples for steady-state signals and 256 samples for transients). Once converted into the frequency domain, the spectrum is split into two components: a "floor" representation that models the broad spectral envelope, and a "residue" vector that captures the fine harmonic details. For binaural audio, the high-frequency HRTF notches fall within this residue layer.

Channel Coupling: Mid/Side vs. Polar Mapping

To maximize compression efficiency, Vorbis employs stereo channel coupling rather than encoding left and right channels entirely independently:

  1. Mid/Side (M/S) Stereo: Used primarily for lower and mid-frequencies where phase relationships are critical. M/S stereo encodes the sum (Mid = Left + Right) and difference (Side = Left - Right) of the channels. Because this operation is mathematically reversible, it introduces no spatial phase smearing, fully preserving low-to-mid ITD cues.
  2. Polar (Phase/Point) Coupling: At higher frequencies and lower bitrates, Vorbis utilizes polar channel mapping. Instead of preserving discrete Left and Right spectra, the encoder transforms the two channels into magnitude and angle vectors. The magnitude represents the overall energy, while the angle represents the stereo position.

The Impact of Polar Coupling on Binaural Imaging

While polar coupling is effective for conventional stereo speaker setups, it presents challenges for high-frequency binaural audio.

By grouping high-frequency MDCT bins into psychoacoustic bands (Bark scale subbands) and quantizing the angle parameter, polar mapping averages spatial information over narrow frequency ranges. In binaural listening, this quantization can smooth out the sharp HRTF peaks and nulls that the brain uses to determine sound elevation and depth. Consequently, sounds that should appear above, behind, or close to the listener may collapse inside the head or sound unnaturally diffuse.

Quality Levels and Discrete Channel Retention

The degree to which Vorbis degrades high-frequency binaural sound depends heavily on the chosen quality setting (-q parameter):

  • Low to Medium Bitrates (below ~160 kbps / -q 5): The encoder aggressively couples high frequencies using polar stereo to save bits. The residue vectors are heavily quantized, causing perceptible loss of high-frequency pinna cues and flattening the binaural image.
  • High Bitrates (~192 kbps to 500 kbps / -q 6 to -q 10): Vorbis restricts or completely disables lossy polar coupling. Instead, it relies on lossless M/S stereo or encodes channels discretely. In this operating range, the fine spectral variations of HRTFs and high-frequency level differences remain intact, delivering transparent binaural reproduction comparable to lossless audio.
