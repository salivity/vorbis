Why Vorbis to Opus Transcoding Degrades Audio

Transcoding audio from Vorbis to Opus leads to generational audio degradation because both formats rely on lossy compression. When a Vorbis file is converted, it is first decoded into an uncompressed stream that already lacks the original data discarded by the Vorbis encoder. Encoding this stream into Opus subjects the audio to a second round of destructive psychoacoustic filtering and quantization, permanently compounding distortion, introducing audible artifacts, and lowering overall fidelity.

The Mechanics of Lossy Transcoding

Both Ogg Vorbis and Opus achieve small file sizes by discarding acoustic information that the human ear is theoretically less likely to perceive, a technique governed by psychoacoustic modeling. When you convert a Vorbis file to Opus, the process follows these steps:

  1. Decoding to PCM: The Vorbis file is decoded into raw, uncompressed Pulse-Code Modulation (PCM) audio. This step does not restore the original quality; it merely decompresses the already degraded audio, along with any compression artifacts inherent to Vorbis.
  2. Re-Encoding to Opus: The Opus encoder analyzes this imperfect PCM stream and applies its own compression algorithms, discarding even more data.

Cumulative Information Loss

Lossy compression is fundamentally irreversible. Vorbis permanently strips high-frequency detail, subtle transients, and quiet sounds masked by louder ones. When the Opus encoder processes the decoded Vorbis audio, it treats the missing data and the subtle artifacts left behind by Vorbis as intentional parts of the source signal.

Because Opus cannot differentiate between genuine musical detail and Vorbis encoding artifacts, it allocates precious bitrate to preserve those flaws. Consequently, both the original sound information and the newly introduced errors are degraded further, creating a compounding effect known as generation loss.

Conflicting Psychoacoustic Models

Vorbis and Opus use different mathematical transforms and psychoacoustic assumptions to handle audio data:

  • Vorbis uses a Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT) with its own masking curves and window-switching logic to manage pre-echo.
  • Opus uses a hybrid architecture: the SILK algorithm (optimized for speech via Linear Predictive Coding) and the CELT algorithm (optimized for music using MDCT with different frame sizes and energy allocation).

Because their models do not align, frequencies that Vorbis considered unimportant might be treated differently by Opus, and vice versa. Opus may attempt to quantize frequency bands that Vorbis has already altered, smearing the sound stage, softening transients, and creating noticeable swishing or metallic ringing (phase and time-domain distortions).

Quantization and Rounding Errors

Every lossy encoding pass involves quantization—the process of rounding continuous values into discrete steps. Doing this twice causes mathematical rounding errors to accumulate. High-frequency clarity diminishes, dynamic range is flattened, and fine ambient details are replaced by audible noise floor rises.

To maintain maximum fidelity, audio should always be encoded directly from a lossless source, such as FLAC or uncompressed WAV, rather than converted between lossy formats.
